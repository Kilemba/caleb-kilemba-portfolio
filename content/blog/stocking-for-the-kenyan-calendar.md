Every December a stationery shop in Nakuru has its best month of the year. Every January
the owner sits on shelves full of stock nobody is buying, having spent December's takings
restocking for a rush that does not come.

He is not bad at his job. He is doing the most natural thing in the world: buying based on
how last month went. In a market where your customers' cash arrives in waves, that is
exactly the wrong instinct — because the month that just ended tells you least about the
month about to start.

## The decision you are actually stuck on

How much do I buy, and when?

Buy too much and your money is sitting on a shelf instead of in your pocket — and in some
lines it is also expiring, fading, or going out of fashion. Buy too little and you send a
customer to the shop next door, who may keep them.

Most owners resolve this by feel, and feel is heavily biased toward the recent past. That
is the whole problem in one sentence.

## The rhythm nobody writes down

Kenyan household spending is not evenly spread. It moves on a few strong, repeating
cycles, and almost every business feels them even if it has never named them:

**School fees.** Three terms a year means three moments when a large chunk of household
money leaves in one go. In the weeks around the start of each term, discretionary spending
tightens sharply — and spending on anything school-related spikes just as sharply. Same
household, opposite direction, depending on what you sell. Check the current year's
calendar rather than assuming, because the dates shift.

**The festive run-up.** Late November into December, money loosens. People buy ahead, buy
bigger, and buy things they have been postponing. Many also travel upcountry, which means
some businesses see a surge and others — especially in Nairobi estates — see their
customers physically leave.

**The January drought.** This is the one that catches people. January combines
post-festive depletion with Term 1 fees. It is frequently the thinnest trading month of
the year, and it arrives immediately after your fattest.

**Month-end and payday.** The last few days of a month and the first week of the next
behave differently from the middle. If you have ever noticed that the middle two weeks
feel dead, that is not your imagination.

None of this is exotic. What is unusual is treating it as a planning input rather than
something that happens to you.

## The data you already have

You do not need market research. You need **last year's own sales, week by week.**

Whatever you have — M-Pesa statement, POS export, even the counter book if it is written
up daily — gives you a date and an amount. That is enough to draw the shape of your own
year.

The critical move, and the one almost nobody makes, is to **compare the same week last
year, not last month.** December to January tells you the obvious. Last January to this
January tells you something you can act on.

## How to read it

Put your weekly totals for the last twelve to twenty-four months in a single column, in
order, and look at the line.

You are looking for three things:

**Where the peaks actually fall.** Not "December" — which week. There is usually a
specific stretch, often the middle two weeks of December, where most of the month's money
lands. Buying for "December" as a block means you carry stock for the quiet half of it.

**How long the trough lasts.** Most owners overestimate how quickly January recovers. If
your line says the thin patch runs six weeks rather than three, your purchasing should
reflect six.

**Whether the shape repeats.** If last January and the January before look alike, you can
plan against it with some confidence. If they look completely different, something else is
driving your business and the calendar is not your main lever — which is also worth
knowing before you plan around it.

A worked shape, using made-up numbers to show what you are looking for rather than to
describe a real shop:

| Week | Last year | This year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early Dec | 180,000 | 195,000 |
| Mid Dec | 340,000 | 370,000 |
| Late Dec | 210,000 | ? |
| Early Jan | 95,000 | ? |
| Mid Jan | 80,000 | ? |

The shop above does not have a December problem. It has a **mid-December** business and a
six-week hangover. Knowing that changes what you buy in November and, more importantly,
what you do with the money December brings in.

## What this unlocks

**Buy for the peak week, not the peak month.** Same total spend, better timing, less
stock stranded in the quiet weeks either side.

**Protect January's cash before December spends it.** This is the single most valuable
habit in a seasonal business. December feels like abundance; a good share of it is working
capital that January will need. Deciding in November how much of December's takings is
untouchable is far easier than deciding in late December when the money is in your hand.

**Buy differently for fee weeks depending on what you sell.** If you sell school-adjacent
goods, that is your rush — stock deep and early, because everyone else is trying to buy
the same thing from the same suppliers at the same time. If you sell anything
discretionary, those same weeks are your trough — hold cash, run your quiet-season
promotions then, not in December.

**Stop paying for a lesson you already learned.** The shop that over-buys every January is
paying an annual fee for not writing down what happened last January.

## The honest limit of this

Last year predicts this year only when conditions are broadly similar. A new competitor
opening nearby, a road closure, a change in your supplier's pricing, or a genuine shift in
the wider economy will all break the pattern — and no amount of history will warn you.

So treat the calendar as your starting assumption, not your forecast. It should shape the
first draft of your purchase order. What you see in the first two weeks of the season
should shape the final one.

## One thing to do this week

Pull your sales for the same eight weeks last year that are coming up now. Total them by
week. Write those eight numbers on one page and put it where you do your ordering.

Then, before your next big purchase, look at the page and ask one question: **am I buying
for the week that is coming, or for the week that just finished?**

That question alone, asked honestly four or five times a year, is worth more than most
stock systems.
