A salon owner has two branches. The town-centre one is always full — chairs busy, phone
ringing, staff run off their feet. The estate one is quieter and she has been thinking
about closing it for a year.

When she finally sat down and worked it out properly, the quiet branch was making more
money.

This is not a trick or a rare case. It is the single most common surprise in multi-branch
retail and services, and it happens for a boring reason: **busy is a measure of activity,
and profit is what is left over.** Those two things come apart as soon as your branches
have different rent, different staffing and different customers.

## The decision you are actually stuck on

You have two or more locations and finite money. You need to know:

- Which one deserves the next investment?
- Which one has a fixable problem?
- Which one, honestly, should close?

Getting this wrong is expensive in a way that compounds. Backing the wrong branch does not
just waste the investment — it starves the one that would have returned it.

## Why revenue misleads you

Comparing branches on takings is the natural thing to do and it is almost always wrong,
because the costs behind that revenue are wildly different.

Town-centre rent might be three times estate rent. A busy branch needs more staff, holds
more stock, and — this is the part people forget — **loses more stock.** Shrinkage, waste,
breakage and simple mistakes all scale with volume and with how rushed your people are.

So the busy branch earns more, spends more, and loses more. Whether it comes out ahead is
genuinely not obvious from the outside, which is why you have to count.

## The question that does the work

Here is the one that cuts through it. Forget accounting categories for a moment and ask:

> **If I closed this branch tomorrow, which costs would actually stop?**

That question separates the costs that belong to a branch from the costs that belong to
the business. Rent on that shop stops. The staff there stop. The stock you buy for it
stops. But your own salary does not stop, the accountant does not stop, and the licence
for the business as a whole does not stop.

What you are left with — the branch's revenue minus only the costs that would disappear
with it — is what that branch genuinely contributes. Everything else is a head-office cost
that has to be carried by the business as a whole.

This matters enormously for the closure decision. A branch can look unprofitable after you
have loaded a share of head-office costs onto it, and still be worth keeping — because if
it closes, those head-office costs do not vanish. They just move onto your other branch.

## What to count

For each branch, over the same three-month period:

**Revenue.** From the same source for both, so you are comparing like with like. If one
branch takes more cash than the other and you only count M-Pesa, your comparison is broken
before it starts.

**Rent and utilities.** Straightforward and usually the biggest difference.

**Staff at that branch.** Wages, plus anything you actually pay on top.

**Cost of goods sold there.** What you paid for what was actually sold.

**Stock losses.** The difference between what should be on the shelf and what is. If you
have never measured this per branch, estimate it honestly for now and start measuring it
properly from this month — it is frequently where the gap between the two branches lives.

Leave out your own drawings, central admin, and anything you would still pay if the branch
closed. Those are real costs, but they are not this branch's costs.

## An illustrative comparison

Made-up figures, to show the shape of the reasoning rather than a real business:

| | Town centre | Estate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Revenue | 900,000 | 540,000 |
| Cost of goods | 380,000 | 210,000 |
| Staff | 240,000 | 110,000 |
| Rent and utilities | 210,000 | 70,000 |
| Stock losses | 45,000 | 12,000 |
| **Contribution** | **25,000** | **138,000** |

The town branch turns over two-thirds more and contributes a fifth as much. Every line
explains a piece of it: higher rent, more staff, and losses nearly four times as large in
shilling terms.

Notice what this does *not* say. It does not say close the town branch. It says the town
branch has a **margin problem, not a demand problem** — the customers are clearly there.
That is a much more hopeful diagnosis, and a completely different action plan.

## Two more numbers worth having

Once you have contribution per branch, two ratios sharpen the picture considerably.

**Contribution per square metre.** This tells you whether you are paying for space you are
not using. A branch with weak contribution but excellent contribution per square metre is
simply too small — the fix might be a bigger unit, not a closure.

**Contribution per staff hour.** This tells you whether you are overstaffed or your people
are stretched. Combined with an hour-by-hour view of when sales actually happen, it
usually points straight at a rota that was set years ago and never revisited.

## What this unlocks

**Invest with evidence.** The branch to back is the one with the best contribution, or the
best contribution per square metre — not the one that feels busiest when you walk in.

**Fix instead of close.** Most weak branches have one dominant cause: rent out of line with
what the location can produce, staffing that does not match the sales pattern, or losses
nobody was counting. Find which of the three and you often have a fixable branch.

**Negotiate from a position.** If a branch's rent is the whole problem, you now know
exactly what rent would make it work. That is a far stronger conversation to have with a
landlord than a general plea.

**Close deliberately, if you must.** And if you do close, you will know what it was
contributing — so you know what the remaining branch now has to absorb.

## The trap to avoid

Do not spread head-office costs across branches and then use that to judge them. It is
fine for understanding overall profit, but it makes a branch look worse than it is for the
closure decision, because those costs do not leave with the branch.

Judge the branch on what disappears with it. Judge the *business* on everything.

## One thing to do this week

Take one page. Two columns, one per branch. Six rows: revenue, cost of goods, staff, rent
and utilities, stock losses, and the total left over at the bottom.

Use three months, not one, and be honest about the losses line even if you are estimating.

Then look at the bottom row — and notice whether it matches the branch you *assumed* was
carrying the business.
