A restaurant owner in Westlands spends 30,000 shillings a month on promotions aimed at
getting new people through the door. Ask her how many of last month's customers came back
this month and she cannot tell you. Not roughly. At all.

That is not unusual, and it is a strange gap, because the two facts are connected: whether
chasing new customers is a good use of 30,000 shillings depends almost entirely on what
happens to them afterwards. If most people come once and never return, she is filling a
bucket with a hole in it — and the promotion is making the hole cost more, not less.

## The decision you are actually stuck on

Every shilling of marketing money goes to one of two jobs:

- **Acquisition** — getting someone who has never bought from you to try.
- **Retention** — getting someone who already has to come back.

Acquisition is almost always the more expensive of the two, and it is where nearly all
small-business marketing money goes, because it is the one that feels like growth.

You cannot sensibly choose between them without knowing your repeat rate. And almost
nobody knows it.

## The data you already have

Here is the part most people miss: **mobile money attaches an identity to a payment.**

When someone pays into your till, the transaction record carries something that identifies
that payer — typically a name, and often a partially masked number. Cash tells you a sale
happened. Mobile money tells you a *particular person* bought something, on a particular
day, for a particular amount.

That is customer data. You did not have to collect it, nobody had to fill in a form, and
it has been accumulating since the day you got the till.

Two honest caveats before you get excited:

**Your cash customers are invisible here.** If a large share of your sales are cash, your
repeat rate from mobile money alone will understate reality. Treat it as a sample, not a
census — the trend is still meaningful even if the level is off.

**One person can look like several.** People pay from different numbers, and family
members share tills. Expect the count to be a bit fuzzy at the edges.

## How to read it

You do not need to build anything. Take three months of statements and answer one
question:

> **How many payers appear more than once?**

Sort the export by the payer column, and count. Two numbers come out of it:

- **Distinct payers** — how many different people paid you.
- **Total transactions** — how many payments there were.

Divide the second by the first and you have **average visits per customer**. Then count
how many payers appear two or more times and divide by the total number of payers — that
is your **repeat rate**.

An illustrative example, with made-up numbers:

| | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Total transactions | 4,200 |
| Distinct payers | 3,100 |
| Payers who came more than once | 740 |
| Repeat rate | 24% |
| Average visits | 1.35 |

Twenty-four percent. Three-quarters of this restaurant's customers came once in three
months and never returned. That single number completely reframes the 30,000 shillings a
month.

## What the number is telling you

There is no universal "good" repeat rate — it depends enormously on what you sell. A
pharmacy and a furniture shop should look nothing alike. What matters is the direction it
moves and what it implies about where money should go.

**A low repeat rate with strong new-customer numbers** means people are willing to try you
and unwilling to return. More acquisition spending makes this worse, not better, because
you are paying to push more people through an experience that is not bringing them back.
The money belongs in finding out why — and the cheapest way is to ask ten of them.

**A high repeat rate with weak new-customer numbers** is the opposite and much happier
problem. What you do works; not enough people know. Acquisition spending here compounds,
because each new customer is worth several visits.

**A repeat rate that is falling** is the most urgent signal on this list, and it is
invisible unless you calculate it more than once. Something changed — a staff member, a
recipe, a competitor, a price. Catching it in the numbers gives you a chance to find out
which.

## A different use of the same number

Once you know average visits per customer, you can finally answer: **what is a customer
worth?**

Average spend per visit, multiplied by average visits, is roughly what a new customer is
worth to you over that window. Now compare that with what you are paying to acquire one.

If a customer is worth 1,200 shillings over three months and you are spending 400 to get
one, the promotion is working. If they are worth 300, it is not — regardless of how many
people it brought in.

This is a calculation most small businesses never make, and it turns marketing from a
matter of taste into a matter of arithmetic.

## Before you message anybody

A serious word, because this is where enthusiasm gets people into trouble.

Having someone's phone number because they paid you **is not the same as having permission
to market to them.** Kenya's Data Protection Act sets real obligations around how personal
data is collected and used, and unsolicited marketing messages are exactly the kind of use
it is concerned with. Beyond the law, it is also a good way to annoy a customer you worked
hard to win.

Analysing your own records to understand your business — counting how many people return,
working out what a customer is worth — is a very different activity from extracting phone
numbers to blast promotional SMS. The first is ordinary business analysis. The second needs
consent, a clear purpose, and a way for people to opt out.

If you want to build a list you can message, build it properly: ask, explain what they will
get, and make leaving easy. A small list of people who agreed outperforms a large one that
did not, and it does not put you at risk.

## What this unlocks

**You stop guessing where marketing money goes.** The repeat rate points at acquisition or
retention, and the two need completely different spending.

**You notice decline early.** A repeat rate checked quarterly is an early warning system
for problems that otherwise surface as a slow, unexplained drift in takings.

**You can justify loyalty efforts, or drop them.** If repeat customers spend more per visit
than first-timers — check, it is not always true — that is a concrete argument for looking
after them.

## One thing to do this week

Export three months of till transactions. Sort by the payer column. Count how many names
appear more than once, and divide by the total number of distinct names.

Write that percentage down with today's date. Do the same thing in three months.

The number itself is useful. The direction it moves is where the real information is.
