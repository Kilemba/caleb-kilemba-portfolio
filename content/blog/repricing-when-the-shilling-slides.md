An electronics shop in Nairobi sells a cable for 800 shillings. It cost 500 when the
owner bought the carton, so he is making 300 — a healthy margin, and he is pleased with
it.

Then he goes to restock and the same cable now costs 620. He is still selling at 800,
still "making 180", and he still feels profitable. But something has quietly gone wrong:
the money coming in from each sale buys less of the next carton than it used to. Sell
enough of them and he will not be able to refill the shelf without dipping into cash from
somewhere else.

He has not made a pricing mistake in the usual sense. He has made a **reference** mistake.
He is pricing against what he paid, and the only number that matters is what it will cost
him to replace it.

## The decision you are actually stuck on

Costs have moved. You know you need to raise prices. But:

- Raise too slowly and you fund your customers' purchases out of your own working capital.
- Raise too fast or too visibly and they go elsewhere, sometimes permanently.

And there is a third fear underneath both: you genuinely cannot tell whether you are
behind, level, or ahead — because nobody is tracking it.

## The one idea that fixes most of this

**Price against replacement cost, not purchase cost.**

Your margin is not the gap between the sale price and what you paid. It is the gap between
the sale price and what you will pay to put that item back on the shelf. If that second
gap is thin, you are slowly liquidating your own stock and calling it profit.

This is the whole article in three lines, and it is the thing most owner-operators never
have said to them plainly. Everything below is just how to act on it.

## The data you already have

You need two numbers per item, and you already have both:

1. **What you currently sell it for.**
2. **What your supplier charges for it today** — not last quarter, today.

The second one is a phone call or a WhatsApp message to your supplier. For most shops,
knowing today's cost on your top twenty items is 80% of the benefit, and it takes an
afternoon to collect.

If you buy imported goods, or goods that someone else imports, add one more habit: note
the date alongside the cost. Over a few months that gives you the rate at which your costs
are actually climbing, which is far more useful than any headline figure — because it is
*your* basket, not the national average.

## How to read it

For each of your top items, work out the margin on replacement cost:

> (selling price − today's cost) ÷ selling price

Then compare it with the margin you *think* you are making, based on what you paid. The
gap between those two numbers is how far behind you have fallen.

An illustrative table — made-up numbers, to show what you are looking for:

| Item | You paid | Costs today | You sell at | Margin you think | Margin you have |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cable | 500 | 620 | 800 | 38% | 23% |
| Adapter | 900 | 950 | 1,400 | 36% | 32% |
| Bulb | 180 | 300 | 400 | 55% | 25% |

The cable and the bulb are the problem, and the bulb is much worse than the cable even
though it is a cheaper item. That is the point of doing this per item rather than
across-the-board: the damage is never spread evenly, and your instinct will not find it.

## Raising prices without losing customers

This is where most advice goes vague. Here is the practical version.

**Not everything is watched.** Your customers know the price of a handful of things —
whatever they buy most often, and whatever is easy to compare. Those are your
*known-value* items. On everything else, most people have only a rough sense.

Move the unwatched items first, and move them further. Move the watched ones last, less,
and more often.

**Small and frequent beats large and rare.** A 5% rise twice a year is noticed far less
than 10% once, even though the outcome is nearly identical. A price that jumps in a single
step is an event; a price that drifts is just the price.

**Change the offer, not only the number.** A slightly smaller pack, a different supplier's
equivalent, or a bundle at a round price all let you restore margin without putting a bigger
number against the same familiar thing. Be straight about it — if a pack is smaller, do not
pretend otherwise. Customers forgive a changed size far more readily than they forgive
being misled.

**Round numbers help.** Moving 380 to 400 reads as tidying. Moving 380 to 395 reads as
squeezing.

**Tell them once, plainly, if it is significant.** "Supplier prices went up in August, so
some of our prices have moved" costs you nothing and buys a surprising amount of goodwill.
Silence invites the customer to invent a worse explanation.

## What this unlocks

**You can restock without borrowing.** The whole point. Each sale funds its own
replacement, which is the difference between a business that turns over and a business
that slowly shrinks.

**You find the items quietly bleeding you.** There are usually two or three, and they are
rarely the ones you would guess.

**You stop repricing in panic.** Once you check replacement cost monthly, price changes
become small and routine instead of a dramatic annual correction that customers notice.

**You know which supplier is moving fastest.** Once you have a few months of your own cost
history, it becomes obvious — and that is a real negotiating position, or a reason to look
at alternatives before it gets worse.

## The honest limits

Replacement-cost pricing tells you what you need to charge. It does not tell you what the
market will pay. If an item's replacement cost has risen past what your customers will
accept, no formula fixes that — your options are a cheaper equivalent, a smaller size,
accepting thinner margin on that line while others carry it, or dropping the line.

Knowing that early is still far better than finding out when the shelf is empty and the
cash is gone.

## One thing to do this week

Pick your ten best-selling items. Message your supplier and get today's price on each one.

Work out the margin on today's cost, not what you paid. Then mark each one: **fine**,
**thin**, or **underwater**.

Move the underwater ones this week — starting with the ones your customers are least
likely to be tracking. That is usually an afternoon's work, and for most shops it is the
highest-return afternoon of the quarter.
