A duka owner in Embu gives credit to about forty regulars. She knows all of them. She
knows who is struggling this month, whose child just started school, who always pays on
Friday.

She also has roughly 60,000 shillings out at any one time and could not tell you which
portion of it is genuinely at risk.

Chamas run the same way. So do plenty of small SACCOs and table-banking groups. Credit
decisions get made on character, history and a feel for the person — and to be fair, that
works far more often than outsiders assume. The problem is not that trust is a bad signal.
It is that trust has no memory for detail, and it does not scale.

## The decision you are actually stuck on

Somebody asks for credit. You have to decide, usually on the spot:

- Do they get it?
- How much?
- What happens if they do not pay, and will I actually do it?

Say yes too easily and your working capital ends up on other people's shelves. Say no to
the wrong person and you lose a good customer, or cause offence in a group where offence
is expensive.

## What you already have that a bank would envy

Here is the thing worth appreciating: **you have repayment history that no formal lender
can see.**

A bank assessing a mama mboga has almost nothing to work with. You have watched the same
person borrow and repay, in some cases for years. In the book under the counter, in your
M-Pesa records, in the chama's ledger, there is a record of exactly how each person has
behaved.

That is genuine credit information. It is simply unstructured — spread across pages,
carried in your head, and never totalled.

## The four things worth writing down

You do not need a scoring model. Four facts per person, kept current, will carry most of
the decision:

**How much they currently owe.** The obvious one, and frequently the only one recorded.

**How long it has been outstanding.** Far more informative than the amount. Two people
owing 3,000 are in completely different positions if one borrowed last week and the other
in March.

**How they have repaid before.** Number of times borrowed, and of those, how many were
cleared on the agreed date. "Usually pays" is not the same as "has cleared eleven of
twelve on time".

**The direction of travel.** This is the one almost nobody tracks, and it matters most.
Someone who used to clear in seven days and now takes twenty-five is telling you something
important — and they will usually be a long-standing, trusted customer, which is exactly
why the pattern gets missed.

That fourth point deserves emphasis. Relationship-based judgment is *systematically* worst
at noticing gradual decline in people you like. The record notices. You do not.

## How to read it

Once a month, write out every person who owes you, with those four facts, and sort by days
outstanding — longest first.

An illustrative list, made-up:

| Person | Owes | Days out | Cleared on time | Trend |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 2,400 | 8 | 14 of 15 | steady |
| B | 5,100 | 46 | 9 of 12 | slipping |
| C | 900 | 5 | 3 of 3 | new, steady |
| D | 11,200 | 63 | 7 of 14 | worse |

Three things jump out that no amount of familiarity would have surfaced.

**D is most of your risk.** Eleven thousand out for two months, and a repayment record of
half. Whatever else you do this month, D is the conversation.

**B is the early warning.** Still a decent record, but slipping and nearly seven weeks out.
This is the one where acting now — a conversation, not a confrontation — usually recovers
the money and the relationship. Left another two months, it becomes D.

**C is being underestimated.** New, small, perfect record. The instinct is to treat a
newcomer cautiously; the evidence says C has earned a modest increase.

## Setting limits before you are asked

The hardest part of informal credit is that decisions get made under social pressure, in
front of other people, with no time to think.

The fix is to decide the rules when nobody is asking. Two are enough for most:

**A cap per person.** Some sensible share of what you take in a month, adjusted by their
record. Written down in advance.

**A cap in total.** The most you are willing to have out at any one time across everyone.
When you hit it, new credit waits for repayments. This single rule is what stops a credit
book from quietly consuming a business's entire working capital.

Having a stated policy also makes refusals far easier to give and to receive. "My limit is
5,000 until the current balance clears" is a rule. "I don't think you'll pay me" is a
judgment about a person, and it is remembered.

## For chamas and groups

The same four facts work, with two additions that matter in a group setting.

**Write the terms down and read them out.** Amount, date due, what happens if it is late.
Most group disputes are not about dishonesty; they are about two people sincerely
remembering different agreements.

**Apply the rules identically.** The moment exceptions are made for some members and not
others, the record stops being a tool and becomes a source of grievance. Consistency is
what makes it fair, and being fair is what makes it last.

## What this unlocks

**You know your real exposure.** Not a vague sense that "a fair bit" is out — an actual
figure, and how much of it is old.

**You chase the right person first.** Sorting by days outstanding puts your attention on
the money most likely to be lost, rather than whoever you happened to see this week.

**You catch decline while it is still recoverable.** Trend is the whole value here, and it
only exists if you write it down more than once.

**You can say yes with more confidence.** This cuts both ways. A good record is a reason
to extend more credit to someone who has earned it — which is worth real money to them and
real loyalty to you.

## A word of caution

This is about keeping better records of your own lending, not about becoming a lender.
Taking deposits, lending at interest as a business, or operating as a credit provider
brings regulatory obligations, and a SACCO has its own governing rules. If you are moving
in that direction, get proper advice.

And be careful with what you record about people. A list of who owes what is ordinary
business record-keeping; it should be kept securely, used for the purpose it was collected
for, and not shared around.

## One thing to do this week

Write out everyone who owes you, with two columns: how much, and how many days since they
took it. Sort by the second column.

You will almost certainly find that a small number of people account for most of what is
out, and that the oldest debts are not the ones you have been thinking about.

Start there. One conversation with the person at the top of that list is usually worth more
than a month of general worrying.
