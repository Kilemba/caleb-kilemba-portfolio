A restaurant signs up to a delivery platform and orders start coming. Monthly revenue is
up. The owner is pleased, the kitchen is busier, and the platform's dashboard shows a line
going up and to the right.

Six months later, the bank balance has not moved.

This is one of the most common and most confusing situations in food service, and it comes
from a simple mismatch: **the platform reports your revenue, and you pay your costs.**
Those two things sit in different places, so unless you deliberately bring them together,
you can grow your turnover and shrink your profit at the same time without ever seeing it
happen.

## The decision you are actually stuck on

You are on one or more platforms. You need to decide whether to:

- stay as you are,
- push harder on your own channel — phone, WhatsApp, walk-in,
- renegotiate your rate,
- or come off entirely.

All four are defensible. Which one is right depends on a number almost nobody calculates:
what you actually keep from one delivered order.

## Why the monthly total cannot tell you

The instinct is to look at what the platform pays out each month and compare it with last
month. That tells you volume, not profit, and it hides the thing that matters — because
the costs of a delivered order are different in kind, not just in size.

You have to go down to **one order**. Not a month, not a week. One typical order, followed
all the way through.

## Everything that comes off a delivered order

Work through these for a single, representative order. Use your own contract and your own
receipts, not general figures.

**The menu price.** Start here. Note whether your delivery menu price differs from your
in-house price — many restaurants raise it to absorb commission, which is fine, but you
need to know which number you are working from.

**Commission.** The platform's percentage. Read your actual agreement; rates vary by
market, by restaurant and by what you negotiated, and the number you remember from signup
may not be the number you are on now.

**Payment and transaction charges.** Sometimes separate from commission, sometimes folded
in. Check.

**Packaging.** Containers, lids, bag, cutlery, the sticker. This is small per order and
completely absent from in-house sales, so it is a genuine extra cost — and for cheap items
it can be brutal as a share of the price.

**Promotions you fund.** This is the big one people miss. When a discount runs, check
carefully who pays for it. If a "buy one get one" or a percentage off is funded by you, it
comes straight off this order, and it may not appear anywhere obvious in the statement.

**Refunds and disputes.** Orders that arrive late, cold, wrong or not at all. Some of that
is charged back to you, depending on the terms and who is judged at fault. Over a month
this is rarely zero, so a share of it belongs on every order.

**Food cost.** What the dish actually costs you to make. If you do not know this per dish,
this exercise is worth doing for that reason alone.

**Waste from timing.** Softer, but real. Preparing for a delivery flow that arrives in
bursts produces more waste than a predictable dining room.

## The thing that quietly does the most damage

Compare what a delivery customer orders with what a table orders.

A table orders a main, then a drink, sometimes a dessert, sometimes a second drink. A
delivery customer orders the main. Drinks are frequently the highest-margin item in a
restaurant, and they largely vanish from the delivered basket.

So the delivered order is not merely the same order with commission taken off. It is
often a **smaller and structurally less profitable basket**, before commission is even
applied. Two things compounding in the same direction is how a business ends up busier and
no better off.

Check this in your own numbers rather than taking my word for it — average basket,
in-house versus delivery, and the share of orders containing a drink. It takes twenty
minutes and it is frequently the single most revealing comparison of the whole exercise.

## What the answer actually tells you

**If the delivered order still contributes something**, delivery is doing a real job. It is
filling capacity you already pay for — the rent and the kitchen staff are there
regardless — and every order above your fixed costs is worth having. Keep it, and stop
worrying.

**If it contributes almost nothing**, you are running a second business for free. That is
where renegotiation matters, and you now have the numbers to have that conversation
properly.

**If it is negative**, every order makes things worse, and volume is actively harmful. This
is rare but it happens, usually on cheap items where packaging and commission together
exceed the margin. The fix is often not leaving the platform — it is removing your lowest
value items from the delivery menu.

That last point is worth sitting with. The decision is rarely all-or-nothing. **Which items
you offer for delivery** is a far more powerful lever than whether you are on the platform
at all, and it costs nothing to change.

## Other levers before you quit

**Set delivery prices separately.** Most platforms allow it. If commission is a fifth,
in-house pricing cannot survive on the platform, and pretending otherwise just means you
pay it.

**Build a minimum order that works.** Small orders are where the fixed costs — packaging,
attention, dispute risk — hurt most.

**Add the drink back.** A meal deal that bundles one is worth trying precisely because the
drink is what the delivery basket is missing.

**Use the platform to earn your own channel.** A card in the bag with your own number is
cheap. A customer who orders directly next time is worth several who do not. Just be
careful to check what your agreement permits — some prohibit exactly this.

## One thing to do this week

Take one order from last week. A normal one, not your best or worst.

On one page, write the amount the customer paid at the top. Then subtract every single
thing that came off it — commission, payment charges, packaging, your share of refunds,
food cost. Whatever is at the bottom is what that order was truly worth.

Do the same for a comparable walk-in order, and put the two numbers side by side.

Most owners have never seen those two numbers next to each other. Whatever you decide
afterwards, you will be deciding with your eyes open.
