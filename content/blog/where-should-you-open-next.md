A bakery owner is shown a unit on a busy road. The agent talks about footfall, about the
new flats going up, about how quickly the last space went. It is a good pitch. It is also
the exact same pitch given to everyone who viewed the unit, and none of it is about her
business.

Meanwhile, sitting in her phone, is three months of delivery orders with the estate name
written on every single one. That is real evidence about where her customers actually are,
and it cost nothing to collect.

## The decision you are actually stuck on

Opening a branch is the largest financial commitment most small businesses make. Deposit,
fit-out, stock, staff — and a lease that does not care whether the branch works.

Get it right and you double the business. Get it wrong and you can lose the original too,
because a failing second branch drains cash and attention from the one that was working.

So the question is not "is this a good location?" It is: **is this a good location *for
me*, given who already buys from me?**

## The data you already have

Three sources, all free, all already in your possession:

**Delivery addresses.** If you deliver at all — even occasionally, even informally over
WhatsApp — you have a record of where your customers physically are. This is the single
most valuable dataset a small business owns for this decision, and almost nobody tallies
it.

**Where people say they are coming from.** If your staff take orders by phone, or you have
ever asked, you have fragments of this. Start writing it down from today; in eight weeks
you will have something usable.

**Your existing branches' patterns.** If you already have two locations, the difference
between them is a natural experiment you have already run. What kind of area, what kind of
customer, what hours — and which one worked.

## How to read it

Tally your deliveries by area over the last three months. Estate, neighbourhood, whatever
unit people actually use. One column of place names, one column of counts.

Then sort it, and look for the thing that matters — which is not the biggest number.

An illustrative tally, made-up:

| Area | Orders | Distance from shop |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Same estate | 420 | walking |
| Estate B | 260 | 15 min |
| Estate C | 185 | 25 min |
| Estate D | 40 | 35 min |

The interesting row is **Estate C**. 185 orders from twenty-five minutes away is not
convenience — nobody travels twenty-five minutes for convenience. Those people are
choosing you despite the distance, which means demand there is real and currently
underserved.

Estate B has more orders but they are close enough that a branch there might simply serve
the same people from a different address. That is the trap, and it has a name.

## The question that separates a good opening from an expensive one

> **Would a branch here bring me new customers, or just move existing ones?**

This is cannibalisation, and it is what turns an apparently obvious expansion into a
disappointment. You open a second shop, it does decent numbers, and then you notice the
first shop is down by almost exactly the same amount. You now pay two rents and two sets
of wages to serve one set of customers.

Distance is your rough guide. Somewhere far enough that your current customers find you
inconvenient is somewhere a new branch adds. Somewhere your customers already happily
travel from is somewhere a new branch mostly relocates.

There is a legitimate version of the second case — if your current shop is genuinely at
capacity, turning people away in the rush, then relieving it has real value. But that is a
capacity decision, and you should make it knowing that is what you are doing rather than
expecting new revenue.

## What to check before you sign

Once your own data has pointed at an area, three checks are worth the days they take:

**Go and sit there.** Twice, at your two busiest hours, on a weekday and a Saturday. Count
people. An agent's footfall claim and an hour with a notebook are not the same category of
evidence.

**Find out who is already serving them.** If there is no competitor, ask why — sometimes it
is genuine opportunity, sometimes somebody tried and left. Neighbouring shopkeepers will
usually tell you which, and it is the cheapest research you will ever do.

**Work out what the branch must take to survive.** Rent plus staff plus your expected cost
of goods gives you a monthly break-even. Then divide by your average sale. If the answer is
a number of daily customers that your own hour-count did not come close to supporting, you
have your answer before you spend anything.

That third check is the one that most often prevents an expensive mistake, and it is
arithmetic you can do on the back of an envelope.

## What this unlocks

**A shortlist based on your customers, not a broker's inventory.** You walk in knowing
which areas your own evidence supports, which changes every conversation that follows.

**A defensible break-even.** You know the number the branch must hit, so you know within
weeks whether it is tracking — instead of hoping for a year.

**A reason to say no.** Most of the value here is in the openings you do not do. An
unremarkable "the numbers don't support it" saves more money than a good opening makes.

**A negotiating position.** If the location only works below a certain rent, you now know
that figure precisely, and you can walk away from anything above it without second-guessing
yourself.

## The honest limits

Your delivery data shows where your *current* customers are. It cannot see demand from
people who have never heard of you, and it is biased toward wherever you have advertised
or been recommended.

So it is very good at confirming demand and very bad at discovering it. Use it to shortlist
and to rule things out, then go and look with your own eyes before you commit. The data
narrows the field; the visit makes the decision.

## One thing to do this week

Take your last three months of deliveries and tally them by area. If you do not record
that, start today — a column in a notebook is enough, and eight weeks from now it will be
the most useful page you own.

Then look for the row that is far away and still substantial. That is where people are
choosing you despite the inconvenience, and it is the most honest signal you will find
anywhere that a branch might work.
